Formalization of Syntactic Theory

Investigating grammatical structure, invariance, and the expressive limits of syntactic models through formal methods.

Hi! Welcome to my research page. I’m Haoyu Wang (王浩宇 [wɑŋ˧˥ xɑʊ̯˥˩ y˨˩˦]). This is my undergraduate research project in theoretical linguistics and formalization. It treats grammars as mathematical objects and asks which of their properties are structural, and what one model of syntax can express that another cannot. I use Lean 4, a functional programming language and proof assistant, to verify formal claims about small grammars.

Bare Grammar and explanatory adequacy

Does generating the right expressions guarantee that a grammar captures the relevant linguistic generalizations?

In Keenan and Stabler’s Bare Grammar (2003), a grammar is a lexicon of basic expressions, each a string paired with a category, together with generating functions that build expressions from expressions. The project constructs overlexicalization examples, grammars that list complex expressions in the lexicon instead of deriving them, to examine the explanatory limits of weak generative criteria. In the limiting case, a grammar that lists a finite set of target expressions and has no generating functions generates exactly that set.

The examples bear on the gap between weak generative capacity and explanatory adequacy, on lexical and rule redundancy, and on whether stronger conditions, such as excluding spurious functions and spurious structure, can tell the two kinds of grammar apart. They were presented at an academic discussion group in July 2026 and are unpublished.

Automorphisms and invariance

Which grammatical properties remain invariant under structure-preserving transformations, and what does this tell us about the grammar?

An automorphism of a grammar is a bijection h on its expressions that preserves the domain of every generating function f and commutes with it: h(f(x1, …, xn)) = f(h(x1), …, h(xn)). Keenan and Stabler take the properties preserved by every automorphism to be the structural ones. Renaming the categories gives an isomorphic grammar with the same invariants, so nothing structural depends on the labels themselves.

Suppose a grammar builds Kim laughed, Kim cried, Lee laughed and Lee cried from two NPs and two VPs by a single rule. Exchanging Kim and Lee is then an automorphism, so category membership is invariant while being the word Kim is not. A grammar that lists the four sentences as lexical items generates the same sentences, but all 24 permutations of them are automorphisms, and it draws no structural distinction among them. This suggests how invariance might separate a generalization from a list. The direction is exploratory, and no new characterization or classification has been proved.

Expressive power of syntactic models

What can one syntactic model express that another cannot, and which theoretical assumptions account for the difference?

This is the project’s main long-term aim, with X-bar theory, Bare Phrase Structure and Minimalist approaches as the models in view. Comparing two grammars differs from comparing classes of grammars, and comparing the strings they generate differs from comparing the structures they assign. A string that separates two grammars does not separate the theories they were written in.

A first Lean-checked pilot (November 2026) applied this to the project’s own grammars. In the Minimalist fragment, the constructor for MERGE records argument order, set a b ≠ set b a. Two objects for Swahili mtoto atalala ‘the child will sleep’ that differ only in this order both converge and externalize identically. The convergent object is therefore not unique under raw equality; whether it is unique up to the order of MERGE is open. No equivalence or separation between syntactic theories is claimed.

Formalization in Lean 4

Bare Grammar is implemented in Lean as data, and a linear grammar, X-bar theory with GB conditions, and a workspace-based Minimalist fragment are instances of the same definition. An earlier version of the X-bar formalization proved endocentricity, that a phrase has the category of its head, as a theorem about trees. A later version indexes trees by bar level and category, so that the type of the head function states the property:

def Head : XTree b c → LexicalEntry c

When a principle is built into the types, violations become unrepresentable, and the principle can no longer be varied within the same representation. This matters when models are compared. An open problem is a representation of unordered MERGE that keeps the distinct copies that Internal MERGE requires.
