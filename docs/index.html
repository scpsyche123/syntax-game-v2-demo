Formalization of Syntactic Theory

Investigating grammatical structure, invariance, and the expressive limits of syntactic models through formal methods.

An undergraduate research project in theoretical linguistics. It treats grammars as mathematical objects: it asks which of their properties are invariant under structure-preserving transformations, compares what different syntactic models can express, and checks small results in the proof assistant Lean 4.

§1

§1 Research Overview

Generative grammars are usually judged by what they generate. This project asks what else a grammar commits us to: which of its properties are structural rather than artefacts of a particular notation, and what one model of syntax can express that another cannot.

Formal methods make these questions answerable, at least in small and exact cases. Once a grammar is treated as a mathematical object (a lexicon together with operations that build complex expressions from simpler ones) one can state precisely what it generates, which transformations preserve its structure, and which assumptions a given property depends on. Claims about small grammars are then checked in the proof assistant Lean 4, so that each is either proved or visibly not.

The project is organized around three questions:

  1. Does successfully generating the right expressions guarantee that a grammar captures the relevant linguistic generalizations? §2
  2. Which grammatical properties remain invariant under structure-preserving transformations, and what does this tell us about the grammar? §3
  3. What can one syntactic model express that another cannot, and which theoretical assumptions account for the difference? §4

The first has been examined through constructed examples. The second and third are open research directions; the third is the project’s main long-term aim. §5 describes the Lean formalization on which all three draw, and §6 a teaching game built from it.

§2

§2 Bare Grammar and Explanatory Adequacy

Does successfully generating the right expressions guarantee that a grammar captures the relevant linguistic generalizations?

Keenan and Stabler’s Bare Grammar (2003) reduces a grammar to its essentials. An expression pairs a string with a category. A grammar consists of a lexicon of basic expressions and a set of generating functions, partial functions that build expressions from expressions. Its language L(G) is the closure of the lexicon under the generating functions. Because no further theory is built into the format, grammars inspired by quite different syntactic frameworks can be stated, and compared, in the same terms.

Weak generative criteria evaluate a grammar by the strings it generates. A grammar can meet such criteria without capturing the generalizations it is meant to express. The project investigates this through overlexicalization: grammars in which complex expressions are listed in the lexicon instead of being derived from their parts. In the limiting case, for any finite set of target expressions, the grammar whose lexicon simply lists them, and which has no generating functions, generates exactly that set while expressing no regularity at all.

The examples raise several related issues:

  • weak generative capacity versus explanatory adequacy;
  • grammars that generate the same expressions but differ structurally;
  • lexical and rule redundancy;
  • finite generation;
  • conditions excluding spurious functions and spurious structure;
  • the potential role of stronger structural constraints.

Status. The overlexicalization examples and their analysis were presented at an academic discussion group in July 2026. They are an analysis of constructed cases, not a published result and not the proof of a new theorem.

Question 2.1. Which stronger structural constraints would distinguish a grammar that captures a generalization from one that merely lists its instances? §3 considers one way of making the difference precise.

§3

§3 Automorphisms and Grammatical Invariance

Which grammatical properties remain invariant under structure-preserving transformations, and what does this tell us about the grammar?

A grammar fixes a set of expressions together with the operations that relate them. Many of its apparent properties, however, belong to the way it happens to be written down: the names of its categories, the identity of particular words, the order in which a notation lists things. The mathematical tool for separating structure from presentation is the automorphism, a transformation of a structure onto itself that preserves all of its operations. Keenan and Stabler build their account on this idea: the structurally significant notions of a grammar are those that its automorphisms leave invariant.

Definition 3.1 (automorphism). Let G be a grammar with language L(G) and generating functions F. An automorphism of G is a bijection h : L(G) → L(G) that fixes every f ∈ F: for all expressions x1, …, xn, the tuple (h(x1), …, h(xn)) lies in the domain of f exactly when (x1, …, xn) does, and then

h(f(x1, …, xn)) = f(h(x1), …, h(xn)).

Under composition, the automorphisms of G form a group, Aut(G).

Definition 3.2 (invariant). A property P ⊆ L(G), or a relation on L(G), is invariant if every h ∈ Aut(G) maps it onto itself.

Invariance has an immediate consequence for labels. Renaming the categories of a grammar, writing X for NP throughout, say, yields an isomorphic grammar, and every invariant carries over unchanged. A property that depends on what a category is called, rather than on how its members combine, is therefore not structural.

Example 3.3. Let G have the lexicon ⟨Kim, NP⟩, ⟨Lee, NP⟩, ⟨laughed, VP⟩, ⟨cried, VP⟩ and a single generating function f, defined on pairs of an NP and a VP, with f(⟨u, NP⟩, ⟨v, VP⟩) = ⟨u v, S⟩. The map σ that exchanges Kim and Lee, and accordingly the sentences built from them, is an automorphism. Writing each expression by its string,

σ(f(Kim, laughed)) = f(σ(Kim), σ(laughed)) = f(Lee, laughed).

No automorphism, by contrast, can send Kim to laughed: Kim occurs as the first argument of f, and laughed never does. In G, membership in a category is invariant; being the particular word Kim is not.

Now let Glist be the grammar whose lexicon lists the four sentences of G as expressions of category S, with no generating functions. Its expressions of category S are exactly those of G, so no criterion based on the sentences generated can tell the two grammars apart. Their automorphism groups can. In G, the relation ‘x and y have the same subject’ is invariant. In Glist, every one of the 24 permutations of the four sentences is an automorphism, including the one that exchanges Kim laughed with Lee laughed and fixes the rest, and that permutation does not preserve the relation. The overlexicalized grammar generates the same sentences while drawing no structural distinctions among them.

This is the sense in which automorphisms may help separate a grammar that captures a generalization from one that lists its instances. Whether invariance-based conditions can do work that weak generative criteria cannot, in grammars of realistic size, is what this direction sets out to investigate.

Question 3.6. When two models represent different kinds of syntactic objects, for instance trees with ordered daughters and sets built by MERGE, what is the appropriate notion of structure-preserving map between them, so that their invariants can be compared? This links the present direction to §4.

Remark 3.7. This direction is exploratory. The examples above are expository. No new group-theoretic characterization of grammatical properties, no classification theorem, and no formal comparison of automorphism groups is claimed. Relevant prior work includes Keenan and Stabler (2003) and Holder (2010). A small Lean-checked instance of symmetry reasoning appears in Observation 4.4.

§4

§4 Expressive Power of Syntactic Models

What can one syntactic model express that another cannot, and which theoretical assumptions account for the difference?

This is the project’s main long-term aim: to investigate the expressive capabilities and limitations of formal syntactic models, potentially including X-bar theory, Bare Phrase Structure and Minimalist approaches. The question becomes precise only once it is settled what is being compared, and by which observations. These comparisons are easily conflated:

Two methodological consequences follow. A string that one grammar generates and another does not separates those two grammars, not the theories in which they were written. And grammars written in different frameworks have to be compared over a shared lexical inventory: setting the project’s Turkish X-bar fragment beside its Swahili Minimalist fragment, for example, would confound differences between theories with differences between lexicons.

A first Lean-checked pilot

In November 2026 the project began testing these distinctions on its own implemented grammars. The facts below concern specific objects in the current Lean model, and each is checked by Lean; the proof file and research notes are listed in §7.

Observation 4.1 (checked in Lean). In the Minimalist fragment, the constructor that represents MERGE records the order of its arguments: set a b ≠ set b a for two distinct roots, and the MERGE rule produces both orders from the same workspace. The definition being modelled, MERGE(P, Q) = {P, Q}, describes an unordered set; the representation does not yet match it.

Observation 4.2 (checked in Lean). For the Swahili sentence mtoto atalala ‘the child will sleep’, there are two distinct objects r1 ≠ r2 that differ only in the order of the daughters of nP. Both are fully labeled, both satisfy the fragment’s conditions at the conceptual–intentional interface, both externalize as mtoto atalala, and both are reached by complete derivations. Uniqueness of the convergent object therefore fails under raw equality of objects. Uniqueness up to the order of MERGE is a different claim, and it is unproved.

Observation 4.3 (checked in Lean). The general Lean definition of a Bare Grammar admits rules whose domain is given by a condition and which take an unrestricted argument. A grammar with a single such rule, whose argument is simply the output string, generates every string. This is a statement about one permissive instance of the general definition, not about the Minimalist fragment. Its point is methodological: comparing unrestricted grammars by string yield cannot distinguish the intended syntactic operations, so restrictions on lexical resources, rule arguments and observations have to be fixed first.

Observation 4.4 (checked in Lean). Consider an unordered pair of two atoms that nothing else distinguishes. Exchanging the atoms leaves the pair unchanged but reverses each of its two linear enumerations; hence no ordering function that lists both atoms can be invariant under the exchange. The result is conditional. It says nothing about linearization in actual Minimalist grammars, where features, labels or interface conditions may break the symmetry. The difficulty itself has a substantial prior literature, and no novelty is claimed.

Question 4.5. Under explicitly stated interface conditions, does every convergent structure built by unordered MERGE admit a linearization that preserves the required word order, labels and occurrence relations? If not, what is a minimal counterexample; if so, exactly which features break the symmetry?

Question 4.6. For two small grammars written in different frameworks over the same lexical inventory, can an inclusion, an equivalence or a separation be proved for a shared set of observations, such as strings, categorial projections, and movement and copy relations?

Remark 4.7. This research is at an early stage. No general equivalence or separation between X-bar theory, Bare Phrase Structure and Minimalist grammars is claimed. There is substantial prior work on the formalization and formal properties of minimalist and related grammars, for example Michaelis (2001), Collins and Stabler (2016) and Schiffer and Maletti (2021), with which the project’s questions have to be connected.

§5

§5 Formalization in Lean 4

Lean 4 is a proof assistant: definitions are written in a typed language, and a statement counts as established only when Lean accepts a proof of it. The project uses Lean to state grammatical definitions exactly and to check claims about small grammars.

Grammars as data

The Bare Grammar library follows Keenan and Stabler’s format. An expression pairs a string with a category; a grammar is a lexicon and a table of rules; a derivation is an inductively defined object; and an expression belongs to the language exactly when a derivation of it exists.

abbrev Expr (Cat : Type) := List String × Cat      -- a string paired with a category

structure BareGrammar where
  Cat     : Type
  lexicon : List (Expr Cat)
  rules   : List (Rule Cat)
  …

inductive Deriv (G : BareGrammar) : Expr G.Cat → Type where
  | lex : e ∈ G.lexicon → Deriv G e
  | app : r ∈ G.rules → DerivList G inputs →
          applyRule G r inputs = some out → Deriv G out
  …

def CanYield (G : BareGrammar) (s : List String) (C : G.Cat) : Prop :=
  Nonempty (Deriv G (s, C))                       -- a derivation exists

Abridged from BareSyntax/Basic.lean; implicit arguments are omitted. The ellipses stand for further fields and constructors, including a second form of rule whose domain is a decidable condition rather than a list of categories, needed when a category is itself a structured object such as a workspace.

Grammars from three periods of generative syntax are instances of this one definition: linear grammars; the X-bar schema with Government-and-Binding conditions (c-selection, Case, the EPP, θ-roles and Move α), produced from parameters, with Turkish setting the head parameter to final; and workspace-based MERGE with a labeling algorithm and interface conditions, for Swahili.

Endocentricity: a theorem, or a type

In X-bar theory a phrase has the category of its head. An earlier version of the X-bar formalization stated this as a property of trees and proved it. The current version builds it into the representation: trees are indexed by bar level and category, and the type of the function that returns a tree’s head states the property.

inductive XTree : Bar → Pos → Type where
  | word     : (e : LexicalEntry c) → Lexicon c e.word → XTree .zero c
  | adjunctL : XTree .two d → XTree .one c → XTree .one c
  | adjunctR : XTree .one c → XTree .two d → XTree .one c
  | compl    : XTree .zero c → XTree .two d → Selects c d → XTree .one c
  | bareX0   : XTree .zero c → XTree .one c
  | bareX1   : XTree .one c → XTree .two c
  | Spec     : XTree .two d → XTree .one c → XTree .two c

def Head : XTree b c → LexicalEntry c

From XSyntax/Tree.lean and XSyntax/Operations.lean in the earlier X-bar repository.

XTree b c is the type of trees of bar level b and category c. A complement can be attached only with evidence Selects c d that a head of category c selects a phrase of category d, and a word can be placed only with evidence that the lexicon licenses it in category c. Head assigns to every tree of category c a lexical entry of the same category c; that Lean accepts this definition is the endocentricity property.

Remark 5.1. The two versions differ in kind. In the first, endocentricity is a theorem about a general representation, one that could in principle have failed for some tree. In the second, an exocentric tree cannot be written down at all: violations are unrepresentable rather than refuted. This suits a teaching tool, but it matters for comparison, since a principle built into the types can no longer be varied, or its consequences measured, within the same representation. Which principles should be types and which should be theorems is a methodological question for §4.

What has been checked

  • X-bar trees: well-formedness by construction, selection and lexical licensing as required evidence, and endocentricity by the type of Head (earlier repository).
  • Proofs that particular strings are not generated by a small linear grammar, for example a very good day in a grammar in which very can never be the input to a rule (earlier repository).
  • The Bare Grammar library, with linear, X-bar/GB and Minimalist grammars as instances. Each level of the Lean version of the game states its goal together with a solution, so building the project checks that the level can be completed.
  • The four observations of §4. The proof file contains no sorry or admit and builds with the pinned toolchain, Lean 4.23.0.
  • The browser demo’s JavaScript transcription of the grammars, compared with Lean’s output case by case.

Open questions in the formalization

Question 5.2. How can MERGE be represented as genuinely unordered, as a quotient of the current objects or otherwise, without collapsing the distinct occurrences that Internal MERGE and copies require? A plain finite-set representation would identify the two copies of a displaced phrase.

Question 5.3. On convergent objects, does externalization factor through the identification of set a b with set b a? On arbitrary objects it does not; for the two known convergent objects of Figure 5 the outputs coincide, so the question remains open.

Question 5.4. Which restrictions on condition-domain rules, such as bounded arguments, a stated provenance for outputs, and an observation function fixed independently of the theory under test, make comparisons by yield informative?

§6

§6 Interactive Syntax Game

The syntax game is a pedagogical application of the formalization. In five short levels the player builds derivations by choosing rules and their inputs; strings, trees and workspaces are computed by the rules and never typed by the player. Each level’s grammar is a Bare Grammar defined in Lean.

  1. A linear grammar. a very good day as an expression of category D, in three steps.
  2. Not in the language. a very day cannot be derived; every move is rejected.
  3. Too strong. With the added rule D N → N (and the words the, this, dog), the grammar derives a a the this dog as an N.
  4. X-bar theory with GB. Turkish kedi köpeği görüyor ‘the cat sees the dog’, with the head parameter set to final, Case, the EPP and Move α.
  5. Minimalist Program. Swahili mtoto atalala ‘the child will sleep’, with workspace MERGE, labeling and interface conditions.

The browser page cannot run Lean. It runs a JavaScript transcription of the Lean definitions, checked against Lean’s output case by case. A version that runs in Lean itself is in development.
